# Portfolio Visual Research

## Scope

The [developer-portfolios collection](https://github.com/emmabostian/developer-portfolios) was used as a discovery source, not a template. The following portfolio sites were reviewed to compare distinct approaches to engineering identity, projects, writing, interaction, and responsive information hierarchy. No code, copy, layout, or visual assets will be copied.

| Portfolio | Focus | What works | What to avoid for Charity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Brittany Chiang](https://brittanychiang.com/) | Frontend engineering | Immediate role, accessible navigation, projects that show stack and outcome, concise scanning | Borrowing the full one-page visual treatment or using experience density Charity has not earned yet |
| [Adham Dannaway](https://www.adhamdannaway.com/) | Product designer and frontend developer | A single, memorable statement of dual identity and crisp navigation | Making the site about design-versus-code rather than Charity's full-stack growth |

## Patterns That Work

1. **Positioning arrives immediately.** A visitor can understand the engineer's role, focus, and point of view within the first viewport.
2. **Projects show evidence.** Strong sites give each project more than a title, logo, and technology list: they show constraints, decisions, links, and a route to deeper reading.
3. **Typography creates hierarchy.** Scale, rhythm, labels, and whitespace are more durable than decorative surfaces.
4. **Personality is specific.** It comes from writing, selection, and composition rather than generic visual effects.
5. **Navigation is calm.** Important routes are obvious; auxiliary content does not compete with work samples.
6. **A portfolio can be progressive.** A fast landing page supports quick scanning, while detail routes carry the engineering narrative.
7. **The visual system matches the evidence.** Highly interactive portfolios work when interaction is itself the person's craft. Technical portfolios work when precision and documentation are visible.

## Patterns to Avoid

- Giant value-proposition headlines that could belong to any developer.
- Repeated uniform cards that make every project feel equally important.
- Skill percentage bars with unsupported claims of mastery.
- Unbounded gradients, glow effects, or frosted cards used as a substitute for composition.
- A gallery that contains a project title, tools, and no account of the actual engineering.
- Hiding real links or contact information behind elaborate interaction.
- Decorative motion that reduces reading comfort or fails reduced-motion users.

## Final Principles for Charity's Portfolio

- Put `Charity Jepkoech` and a precise full-stack direction first; keep the language aspirational but evidence-based.
- Make the project gallery the visual centre of the page using uneven editorial compositions, clear status, and restrained project-specific gradient atmospheres.
- Carry each substantial project into a real route with a readable case study, technical decision record, links, and ordered screenshots.
- Use a dark modern public surface with restrained violet, purple, and pink accents. Avoid generic dark/cyan dashboard styling, gradient overload, and traditional editorial-print treatments.
- Keep the owner CMS private and utilitarian. It serves content maintenance; it does not need to mimic the public site's visual language.
- Preserve accessibility, keyboard navigation, readable contrast, and reduced-motion support as design constraints rather than finishing touches.
